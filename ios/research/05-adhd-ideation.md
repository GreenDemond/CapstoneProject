# More ideas: ADHD-skill ideation run

A wider brainstorm using the `adhd` ideation skill. Ideas only; nothing is built.

**How this was produced.** Seven separate AI runs each generated ideas under a different frame, without seeing each other. A critic run then scored and grouped them, three runs bred hybrids, and three runs deepened the top picks. The earlier ideas in `04-design-and-gamification-ideas.md` were banned so the runs had to go past them.

**How to read the scores.** `[N V F]` are a model critic's 0–10 ratings for novelty, viability in a three-week beginner build, and fit. They are opinions, not evidence. Where an idea connects to the research in `01-evidence.md`, a note says so.

**Departures from the skill's recipe.** Scoring and clustering ran as one call instead of two, and the optional second round was skipped. Total: 13 isolated runs.

---

## 1. Brief

**Problem.** Design and game ideas for a web app where a student with ADHD pastes an assignment, gets it split into small steps, and is coached through them, with daily and weekly goals.

**Banned as obvious:** micro-steps with a progress bar; streaks and points; Pomodoro timer; unlockable cosmetics; celebration animations; and all 14 headline ideas from file 04.

**Frames used:** speedrunner, 10-year-old, biology, inversion, pre-digital trades, plus a random-stimulus run and a questions-only run.

**Random stimulus:** a climbing wall.

## 3. Converge

### Shortlist

**★ 1. The comeback screen.** After a day or more away, the app opens to one button, "pick up here", showing the unfinished step already cut down to its smallest slice. There is no dashboard and no missed-goal count.
- Why: highest-scoring idea in the run [N7 V8 F9], and its hybrid adds the shrunk slice.
- Evidence link: none direct. It targets the return after a lapse, which no study in the review measured. It is a design bet.

**2. "I drifted" earns a smaller step.** Honesty has an immediate, visible payoff.
- Why: best fit score [F9]. It solves the problem that students will not report drifting if reporting gains them nothing.
- Evidence link: supports self-monitoring, the best-evidenced feature in `01-evidence.md`.

**3. The pet rock.** One deadpan object that moves a little per step toward the edge by the end of the week.
- Why: the cheapest possible game layer, and its tone is the opposite of every other reward app.
- Evidence link: weak. It is a progress display with personality.

**4. Take the question seriously: "What if small steps make the work feel longer?"** Highest-scoring item overall [N8 V8 F9].
- Why: it challenges the core feature. A 12-step list can look worse than the original assignment.
- What it changes: let the student choose how many steps (3, 5, or 8), and show only how many remain in the current chunk.

### Traps

| Idea | Why it is a trap |
|---|---|
| Credit for each sub-step the student creates | Invites endless splitting to farm credit. *My note: letting the student split is still good and matches the evidence; the trap is paying per piece.* |
| "Dyno" leap with a reset on failure | The reset can feel like punishment. |
| Steps that fade and sprout | Hard to build, and a shifting screen may disorient. |
| Ship's bell | Sound is unusable in class or a library. *Counting time up instead of down is still worth keeping, silently.* |
| Varying each step's look; breathing background | May overload students sensitive to visual change, or become the distraction. |
| Classmates-online glow | Needs a server and live presence data. |
| Distraction memory with counter-moves | Too large for three weeks. |
| Credits-and-debits ledger | A "losses" column reads as shame. |
| Timed recall races | Speed pressure can cause anxiety. |
| Habit-for-hint trade | Cannot be verified, and it puts a toll on getting help. |
| Recorded encouragement from another person | Storing someone else's voice raises consent questions. |
| Points for admitting a fake assignment | Rewards an edge case. |
| Speech-to-text notes | Unreliable across browsers; microphone permission adds friction. |
| Fear map versus crux, and its two follow-ons | Marking up pasted text is a hard interface to build, and the pattern needs more history than a three-week beta gives. *Worth revisiting later; see section 5.* |
| Rock rewinds, then leaps | Nets more progress for drifting than for not drifting. |

## 4. Focus: the top three, deepened

### ★ The comeback screen

**How it works.** When the student leaves mid-step, the app saves a bookmark. If they return after a day or more, it skips the normal home screen and shows one card: a short friendly line, the unfinished step cut to its smallest slice ("re-read this one paragraph"), and one large button. Finishing the slice drops the student into the normal step flow. After three or more days the slice gets smaller and the tone warmer. Missed-goal information exists only if the student goes looking for it.

**Load-bearing risk.** Choosing the slice. Too trivial feels patronizing; too large defeats the purpose. It runs at the most fragile moment, so a bad slice costs trust.

**First step.** On paper, mock up five comeback screens from real assignments, varying slice size and tone. Show them to three students and ask which they would actually tap after three days away.

**Branches.**
- After the slice, offer "5 minutes" or "the full step", so the student sets the commitment.
- Make the slice a recall prompt ("What do you remember about X?"), so coming back doubles as spaced retrieval. *This ties the idea to the retrieval-practice evidence.*
- Count comebacks instead of consecutive days.
- If the student tapped "I drifted" and then left, acknowledge both on return: noticing and coming back.

### "I drifted" earns a smaller step

**How it works.** Tapping "I drifted" splits the current step in two and shows the first half with a line such as "Good catch. Smaller target, same credit." The progress bar gains notches so the smaller pieces still count. Repeated taps keep shrinking steps down to a floor of about one minute, at which point the app suggests a short movement break. After a few adapted steps are finished, step size quietly returns to normal.

**Load-bearing risk.** Students tap "I drifted" in advance to keep every step tiny. The design questions are where the floor sits and how fast size returns to normal.

**First step.** On paper, mock up three screens (normal step, drift tap, adapted step). Have two classmates talk aloud through them. Ask whether "adapted" feels helpful or patronizing, and whether they would tap honestly.

**Branches.**
- Bonus only for finishing an adapted step *and then* a normal-size step. This rewards the recovery and discourages farming.
- After a drift tap, ask "What do you remember from the last step?" before showing the smaller one.
- After three drift taps in a session, offer a short reset: stretch, name one thing you remember, set a tiny goal.
- If a step runs far longer than expected with no tap, the app offers to split it. This catches students who forget to report.

### The pet rock

**How it works.** A small gray rock sits at the left of a track. Each finished step moves it slightly right. The track length matches the week's total steps, so finishing everything puts the rock at the edge. At the end of the week it drops off with a plain "plop" and a new track starts; if it did not make it, it stays where it is, with no comment. Now and then a dry caption appears ("rock is thinking about math"). The whole reward takes under two seconds.

**Load-bearing risk.** The joke wears off. Minimalism is charming for a week or two, and adding features to fix that would ruin the tone.

**First step.** On paper, walk the rock through five kinds of week: few steps, many steps, two missed days, and so on. Check that the movement is visible and satisfying in each.

**Branches.**
- A bank of 30 to 40 rotating deadpan captions, which is cheap novelty.
- The rock changes material each completed week: pebble, granite, marble, obsidian.
- Finished rocks line up on a shelf as a personal record.
- The rock gives a small wobble when the student answers a recall prompt.

## 5. Provocation

**Start from fear, not from the assignment.** Several ideas circled the same point: the obstacle is often dread, not the size of the task. The trapped hybrid "fear map versus crux" is the sharpest version. The student marks what they dread before starting and flags what was actually hardest afterward. Over months, the app could show them that the two rarely match. That is too large for this build, but it could be the project's "future work" section, and a one-question version fits now: before starting, "Which part are you dreading?"; after finishing, "Which part was actually hardest?"

---

## 6. What I would add to the three-week plan

Ranked by value for effort. All of this is judgment.

1. **Comeback screen.** Small to build if the slice is simply "re-read the step and tap go". Do the paper test first.
2. **"I drifted" splits the step.** Reuses the assignment-splitting feature already planned.
3. **Let the student choose the number of steps.** Answers the "small steps feel longer" question at almost no cost.
4. **Pet rock as the progress bar.** Replaces the quest map or build-a-picture from file 04; pick one of the three.
5. **Dread and crux questions.** Two taps per assignment, and they give the beta something interesting to report.
